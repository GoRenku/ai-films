## Why this film matters

Iceland, June 1863. Professor Lidenbrock, his nephew Axel and their guide Hans wait in the crater of Snaefells for the noon shadow to touch the right chimney. In this version their old map has no words, only drawings, among them a pale salamander with blue spots, and a rival professor with an umbrella and a cheerful porter is going down too. Below the dry galleries waits a sea under a stone sky, a five-metre blind salamander called Glim, two sea giants, a storm and a shore of giant bones.

The 39-minute film is a careful adaptation. The release lists what comes from Verne and what the studio invented, and the director's note explains the central change: Verne's professor follows a message in runes, but here the route is read from pictures, the same way the audience reads it. Glim is modelled on the olm, the real blind cave salamander of the Dinaric karst, scaled up and given rows of blue lights.

## How it was made

Thousand Words FilmS wrote the original story and screenplay and discloses AI-generated visuals and voices, with music from the YouTube Audio Library. Its note describes one editing decision in detail: the chapter cards were first named for what happens in them, then renamed for places on the route, so the screen tells the viewer where they are and never what is coming. The studio's continuing series [The Clay Legion](/films/the-clay-legion/) uses the same titled-chapter structure.
