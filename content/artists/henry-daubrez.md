## Why follow this artist

[KITSUNE](/films/kitsune/) and [BANISHED](/films/banished/) offer two different emotional registers: tenderness and vengeance. The continuity between them is deliberate art direction.

Daubrez also publishes unusually useful process details. His disclosures show the work of writing, selecting, editing and designing sound around generated footage. That helps explain where judgment enters the process, beyond a list of products.

## JUNKYARD KING — Chapter 3 release

On October 6, Daubrez announced Chapter 3 for **Wednesday, October 7 at noon ET** and described its accompanying images as screengrabs from the finished film. Asked about the team on this chapter, he said it was mostly his own work with help from one person. That is an episode-specific account, not a verified total credit roll or a claim about the whole series.

[Chapter 3: The Other Side](/films/junkyard-king-the-other-side/) was released on October 7. The creator-linked 4K YouTube episode is now playable, with the original series description and chapter-specific support disclosure. The film record distinguishes named collaboration from exact roles, and production support from a verified model inventory.

## Generating compositions for posters

In his [October 9 process note](https://x.com/henrydaubrez/status/2108597554766385423), Daubrez names **Seedance 2.5** as a tool for poster exploration: provide characters and environment, request varied cinematic compositions, then scrub the generated video and extract a selected frame. This is his disclosed approach to promotional images, not a film-wide model credit or a guarantee of consistency.

## A tool for planning shots before generation

In an [October 10 statement](https://x.com/henrydaubrez/status/2108962744246653127), Daubrez says he worked on a tool to automate part of his storyboarding process. His [October 11 demonstration](https://x.com/henrydaubrez/status/2109160952348422353) and [direct technical reply](https://x.com/henrydaubrez/status/2109173754563895700) make that account more concrete: he uses **Claude Code and Opus** to build a previsualisation tool running on **Three.js**, creates scenes individually, and renders them into storyboard frames. The demonstration post names **Opus 5.5**.

The useful distinction is between designing the relationships among shots and asking a video model to invent them. Daubrez describes retaining that scene-planning step in a tool he is building. Consistent shot logic is his stated aim, not an independently measured result. The posts do not establish which released episode used the tool, the model used to turn a blockout into finished imagery, or any measured time or cost saving. These are development disclosures, not retrospective film-wide credits.
